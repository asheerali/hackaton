The answer in one paragraph

For the whole 30 minutes, not one device could log in to the corporate Wi-Fi. 63 devices (30 laptops, 33 phones) tried 654 times on 26 access points across all 8 channels. Every time, the access point asks "who are you?", nothing comes back from the network side, it asks again after 30 s, and after 60 s it throws the device out ("802.1X authentication failed"). Devices keep retrying, waiting longer each time. After 3 to 5 failures, 18 laptops are put on a fast-reject list and bounced within seconds of every attempt. Meanwhile the tools network on the same radios works perfectly. This is one site-wide problem in the login service behind the Wi-Fi (RADIUS or the path to it), not a radio problem. It creates a probe storm that wastes airtime for everyone. A real-time version of our tool would flag it 36 seconds after the first failed login.

2 How everything was verified

  • Two independent decoders agree. Your scapy converter and Wireshark's tshark were compared on all 28,536 overlapping frames: every field matches (time, rate, channel, signal, type, flags, addresses, sequence, status and reason codes, EAP, EAPOL message numbers). The only difference is by design: tshark reports the on-air length of the 171 truncated data frames.
  • Security types read from the air, not guessed. Beacons advertise NET-C with AKM 1 (802.1X) and NET-T with AKM 2 (pre-shared key).
  • Codes checked against the standard. All reason, status and EAP meanings come from Wireshark 4.6.9's tables (IEEE 802.11-2020 and later, RFC 3748), saved in catalog/ieee_codes.json.
  • Every quoted number is re-computed by verify_findings.py: 36/36 checks pass. My draft evidence had three wrong numbers (22 APs, 1.01 M beacons, 5 IoT devices); the checks caught them, and they are corrected to 26, 914,057 and 4.
  • Real time was measured, not estimated. realtime_bench.py replays the real recordings into live tshark pipes (see section 4).
Signs this is an emulated scenario: every device's first attempt falls on an exact 2-second schedule; signal strength never changes; 5 GHz beacons at 1 Mbit/s. Protocol logic transfers to real sites; airtime maths from this data does not.

3 What is wrong, and where

Critical F1. Corporate login never gets past "who are you?"

Seen: each attempt goes: knock (accepted) → sign in (accepted) → the access point sends "EAP Identity request" → no next question, no success, no failure, no key messages. The access point re-asks every 30.0 s and kicks the device at 60.0 s with reason 23 ("802.1X authentication failed"). 654 attempts, 63 devices, 26 access points, 8 channels, 0 successes.

Why the network side: for the 12 devices the sensors can hear, the device answers its identity within 1 ms, every time; the next step belongs to the access point and the login server, and it never comes. On the tools network, same radios, the access point accepts the device's key (M3) in 31 of 31 handshakes seen. A radio or device fault would not hit 26 APs and two device types identically.

Most likely causes, in the order an engineer should check: RADIUS/NPS server down or unreachable from the controller; wrong shared secret after a change; firewall/VLAN change on the controller-to-RADIUS path; RADIUS overloaded. Headers alone cannot tell these apart; the RADIUS and controller logs can.

High F2. The retry loop: devices spend the whole 30 minutes failing

Each corporate device made 8–14 attempts (median 10). The wait between a kick and the next try grows from a median of 28 s in the first 5 minutes to ~200 s after 20 minutes, so devices are offline and, over time, try less and less. This is the "deauth loop" from the brief; the tool links it to F1 instead of reporting 63 separate problems.

High F3. New: 18 laptops are put on a fast-reject list

After 3–5 slow kicks, 18 laptops (each on its own access point, all among APs 1–22) switch to a different treatment: the access point sends "previous authentication no longer valid" (reason 2) within a median of 8 s of every rejoin, and keeps repeating it every 11–15 s (as a deauth) or ~26 s (as a disassociation). 1,477 such frames. It starts one laptop at a time, about every 15 s, from 6.7 to 10.7 minutes; none of these laptops gets a reason-23 kick after its switch. The frames carry the access point's own running sequence numbers, so they are genuine, not spoofed.

Plant meaning: this matches a controller "client exclusion / block-list after repeated 802.1X failures" policy. Important for the fix: repairing RADIUS will not bring these 18 laptops back until their exclusion is cleared or times out. Why only these 18 (and not the other 45 devices with more failures) cannot be seen in headers; it is a question for the controller configuration.

High F4. Probe storm: devices search non-stop, a third of replies are lost

173,964 probe replies in 30 minutes (mean 96.5/s, peak 217/s). 31.3% had to be re-sent because the device did not acknowledge, usually because it had already moved on to scan the next channel. Almost every retry in the data (54,484 of 54,544) is a probe reply. It reaches the healthy IoT devices too (3,049 replies). Fixing F1 should end it; if it does not, it is a separate problem.

Medium F5. Missing networks

Every AP should offer both networks. Missing for the full 30 min: NET-C on AP03, AP13, AP16, AP17; NET-T on AP06, AP13, AP28 (AP13 is not heard at all). The 8 devices "homed" on those APs (L-03, L-13, L-16, L-17, H-08, H-11, H-12, H-28) appear in 2,000–3,100 probe exchanges each and never join. Could be a disabled network or an AP on a channel no sensor covers: check the controller.

Medium F6. Slow beacons

All 914,057 beacons are marked 1 Mbit/s, a rate that does not exist on 5 GHz. If real, adverts alone use 10–19% of each channel (19% on ch 36/40 with 9 networks). At 6 Mbit/s it would be 1.7–3%. Plant rule: minimum basic rate 12–24 Mbit/s and at most 2–3 networks per AP.

Info F7. What sensors can and cannot hear

Access-point replies outnumber device scans 29 to 1 (173,964 vs 6,024). Only 12 of 63 corporate devices and 4 of 24 IoT devices are heard during their own join exchange. Beacon loss is 1.7–2.3% everywhere and no sensor ever went deaf. So the tool must judge from the access point's side and never treat "not heard" as "did not happen". Example: one IoT join shows key message 1 but not 3, yet the AP sets up Block-Ack 60 ms later: connected, frame just not captured.

Healthy F8–F9. Watch items and the good news

15 devices (laptops, phones, IoT) said "I'm leaving" within the first 9 s; one more at 12.4 min. On its own that is normal; as a burst it suggests a shared trigger. The tools network is healthy: 24 IoT devices, 31 handshakes reach M3 (M1→M3 in 0.3–2.5 ms), no key, MIC or group-key failures. No device roamed and no access point ever refused a knock or sign-in (all status codes 0).

Several sensors, one story. Each sensor listens on its own channel and hears its own access points; no network is heard by two sensors, so frame-level de-duplication finds nothing here. The link between sensors is the pattern: the same failure signature on 26 access points across all 8 sensors = one root incident with 63 affected devices, not 63 or 654 incidents. Sensor start times differ by up to 2.3 s, so cross-sensor matching allows several seconds of slack.

6 The error catalog (Part 3)

catalog/error_catalog.json (machine-readable) and error_catalog.md (readable) are generated by catalog/build_catalog.py. Each entry has: plain meaning, what it looks like in headers, detection rule and thresholds, the normal behaviour it must not be confused with, likely causes, how to confirm outside the headers, standard fix, owner team, related IEEE codes, and whether it appears in this data (with evidence).

The OTHER rule: anything no entry matches is kept, never dropped: an unmapped or reserved code (OTHER-CODE), a sequence the device-story machine does not define (OTHER-SEQ), or a metric jump no detector explains (OTHER-STAT). These go to unknown_events.jsonl with ±10 s of context, and the discovery agent turns clusters of them into draft entries. Draft detection rules are written in a small JSON rule language (fields, time window, group-by, threshold) rather than free code, so they can be checked automatically and back-tested before a human approves them.

8 What to do next

  1. Build Part 1 as a streaming pipeline on json_full/ replay first, then on live pipes (skill file updated with the verified facts and the real-time design).
  2. Wire detectors to catalog ids so every incident has a name, a meaning and a standard fix from day one.
  3. Build the Part 2 agent with read-only tools over the incident store and catalog; start with F1 as the test case.
  4. Add the OTHER store now, even before the Part 3 agent exists, so unknown events are kept from the first run.
  5. Pitch opener: "0 of 654 corporate logins, tools network fine on the same radios, flagged in 36 seconds: it's the login server, not the Wi-Fi."
